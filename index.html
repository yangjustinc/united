What is UNITED?

Each year, more than 300,000 adults in England get help from drug and alcohol treatment services. Every one of those services records information about the people it supports in the same national data system: the National Drug Treatment Monitoring System (NDTMS), held by the Office for Health Improvement and Disparities.

NDTMS is the only nationally representative record of drug and alcohol treatment in England. It is used to plan and fund services, track trends and evaluate treatment. But it doesn't work as well as it could:

  • staff find it difficult and time-consuming to complete, which takes time away from care
  • researchers and services can't easily access the data
  • it doesn't capture what matters most to people in recovery

UNITED was a 12-month project to find out how NDTMS could be collected and used better, working throughout with Change Grow Live, Turning Point and people with lived experience of treatment.

Why it matters

For people using services
Better data can help services respond to individual needs, track recovery in ways that mean something, and offer more personalised support.
For services and staff
Simpler systems free up time for care and give better information for improving treatment.
For public health
Better monitoring can pick up changes in drug and alcohol use sooner, so care can respond sooner too.

How we did it

Three work packages, with people with lived experience involved throughout.

  1. Work package 1

    Scoping review

    We reviewed every study we could find that used NDTMS data, to understand its strengths and weaknesses for research, and looked at how other countries monitor treatment.

  2. Work package 2

    Qualitative study

    We interviewed 20 staff and 12 people in treatment, and carried out four observations inside treatment services, across the South West, North East and London.

  3. Work package 3

    Knowledge mobilisation workshops

    Online and in-person workshops brought people in treatment, providers, charities and policymakers together to co-create recommendations and plan future research.

  4. Throughout

    Working with people with lived experience

    People who use or have used drug and alcohol services shaped our questions, reviewed our materials, helped interpret findings and co-created recommendations.

What we learned

From our scoping review of NDTMS research. Full details are in the outputs.

Key figures from the scoping review
83studies used NDTMS data between 2005 and 2026: 45 journal articles, 34 government outputs and 4 doctoral theses.
30%of these linked people's NDTMS records to other data, such as hospital, mortality or justice records, to answer questions NDTMS can't answer alone.
11%reported involving patients, the public or practice partners in the research.
28%made their data or code available, and 2.4% registered a protocol in advance.

From our interviews and observations: people in treatment, frontline staff, provider organisations, OHID and local authorities often see NDTMS differently. They differ on how, why and what data are collected, and on how those data can be used and linked to other datasets.

Terms we use

NDTMS
National Drug Treatment Monitoring System. The national dataset that publicly commissioned drug and alcohol treatment services in England contribute to.
OHID
Office for Health Improvement and Disparities, part of the Department of Health and Social Care. It holds and manages NDTMS.
CPRD
Clinical Practice Research Datalink. Anonymised GP records from practices across the UK, used for research.
Data linkage
Joining one person's records across different datasets, such as treatment and hospital records, while protecting their identity.
Lived experience
Direct, personal experience of drug or alcohol use or treatment, past or present.
Scoping review
A structured search of published research to map what has been studied and where the gaps are.
